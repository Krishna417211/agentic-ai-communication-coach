# Presenting and public speaking

## Open with a hook, not an agenda
The first thirty seconds decide whether the room listens. Open with a concrete
number, a short story or a question the audience actually cares about. Reading
out an agenda slide is the fastest way to lose a room.

## One idea per slide, one message per talk
If your audience remembers exactly one sentence, what should it be? Write that
sentence first, then build only the material that supports it. Every slide that
does not serve that sentence should be cut or moved to an appendix.

## Write for the ear
Spoken sentences must be shorter than written ones — around 12 to 15 words.
Listeners cannot re-read a subordinate clause. Prefer concrete nouns and active
verbs, and signpost transitions out loud: "that is the problem; here is what we
did about it".

## Manage your pace and pauses
Nerves speed you up. A comfortable presentation pace is about 130 to 150 words a
minute, so a ten-minute talk is roughly 1,300 to 1,500 words. Pause deliberately
after your key sentence — silence makes the point land and gives you time to
breathe. Fillers ("um", "like", "you know") are usually swallowed pauses; the fix
is to be comfortable with two seconds of silence.

## Handle questions well
Repeat or paraphrase the question so the whole room hears it and you buy thinking
time. If you do not know, say "I don't know, I'll find out and come back to you"
and then actually do. Bluffing is obvious and it costs you the rest of the Q&A.

## Rehearse out loud, standing up
Reading through slides silently is not rehearsal. Say it aloud, timed, at least
twice. The second run is where you find the sentences that only work on paper.
