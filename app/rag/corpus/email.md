# Writing emails people actually answer

## Put the ask in the first two lines
Busy readers decide whether to keep reading in about three seconds. Open with the
request or the news, then give context. "Burying the lede" — opening with three
sentences of background before the ask — is the single most common reason an
email gets left unread in the inbox.

## Subject lines
A good subject line is specific and under 60 characters, because that is where
most clients truncate. Prefer "Budget approval needed by Thursday" over "Quick
question". If the email needs an action, say so in the subject. If it is purely
informational, prefix it with "FYI" so the reader knows they can skim it.

## One email, one ask
If you need three things, you will reliably get one. Either split into separate
emails or use a numbered list and ask explicitly for a response to each item.
Numbered lists get answered; paragraphs containing three buried questions do not.

## The call to action
Every email that needs a reply must end with who does what by when. "Let me know
your thoughts" is not a call to action — there is no deadline, no named owner and
no definition of done. "Could you confirm by Thursday 5pm whether the budget is
approved?" is.

## Length
Under 200 words for routine email. Anything longer belongs in a document with the
email as a two-line summary and a link. If you cannot state your ask in five
sentences, you do not yet know what you are asking for.

## Following up
Follow up once after three working days, on the same thread, adding new
information rather than repeating yourself. A follow-up that just says "bumping
this" reads as pressure without value. A follow-up that says "we now have the
Q3 numbers, which changes the picture" earns its interruption.
